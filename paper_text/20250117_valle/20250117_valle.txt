Abstract
Intracortical microstimulation (ICMS) of somatosensory cortex evokes tactile sensations whose 
properties can be systematically manipulated by varying stimulation parameters. However, 
ICMS currently provides an imperfect sense of touch, limiting manual dexterity and tactile 
experience. Leveraging our understanding of how tactile features are encoded in the primary 
somatosensory cortex (S1), we sought to inform individuals with paralysis about local geometry

Introduction
Touch conveys rich information about our interactions with objects that facilitates dexterous 
behaviors(1). When we touch and manipulate an object, we identify its textural qualities 
and local geometric features. This information is consolidated into a three-dimensional 
representation of the object in a process known as stereognosis(2). This is enabled, in 
part, by neurons in primary somatosensory cortex (S1) that have receptive fields tuned for 
these features(3). Spinal cord injury often disrupts both motor and sensory functions of the 
hand(4). This loss can be partially circumvented by restoring hand and arm movements 
using robots controlled by motor intent signals decoded from motor cortex(5); however, 
normal dexterous hand function is impossible without somatosensation. Intracortical 
microstimulation (ICMS) of Brodmann’s Area 1 (BA1), a subregion of S1, has been shown 
to evoke vivid tactile sensation seeming to originate from the skin (projected fields, PFs)(6–
8) and provides a promising avenue for restoring somatosensory feedback(9–11).
Initial ICMS attempts have focused almost entirely on two features: sensation location(6–
8, 12, 13) and intensity(10, 14). ICMS can be used to intuitively signal the location of 
object contact on a bionic hand by linking sensors on the hand to electrodes in BA1 which 
evoke somatotopically-matched sensations(9). The perceived intensity, controlled by ICMS 
amplitude(6) or frequency(11) can be used to signal interaction force. However, the sense of 
touch is far richer than these two components. It also conveys information about textures, 
material properties, local contours, and motion of objects across the skin. These aspects of 
touch are critical for our body perception, and ability to dexterously interact with objects.
Here, we investigated whether ICMS could encode local geometrical features of touch 
(Fig.S1A). We leveraged the fact that overlapping PFs created with multi-electrode ICMS 
(mICMS) combine additively(12) to create composite PFs with specific shapes. We 
temporally patterned mICMS to evoke a sense of motion across the skin. Together, this 
spatiotemporal patterning expanded the repertoire of artificial touch beyond the basic 
features.
Spatial patterning of ICMS evokes tactile shape percepts.
We performed a series of experiments in two participants (c1-c2) with cervical spinal 
cord injuries, using microelectrode arrays implanted in the hand representation of BA1 
(Fig.1A,B). In these experiments, we identified combinations of three electrodes with 
spatially-aligned PFs which we hypothesized would evoke edge-like sensations. Indeed, 
the participants, without knowledge of the intended goal of the experiment, spontaneously 
reported feeling edges with specific orientation and shape (MovieS1). To test the robustness

of this phenomenon, we repeated this paradigm with various PF configurations (Fig.2A) and 
found that both participants could reliably discriminate the edge orientation regardless of the 
tested digit (Fig.2B, c1:81-89-84% on d1-d2-d3, n=150; c2:61–68% on d2-d3, n=75; p<0.05 
for all).
Next, we assessed the effect of ICMS duration and amplitude on orientation recognition. 
Consistent with natural touch(15, 16), increased duration improved orientation recognition, 
reaching asymptotic performance at 0.5 seconds (78%, n=225, p<0.05) (Fig.S2A). This 
implies that this restored feature is sufficiently rapid to enable real-time feedback in bionic 
devices(17). Unlike duration, there was no effect of amplitude on discriminability (Fig.S2B, 
n=270, p>0.05), also consistent with natural touch(18). Thus, the spatial properties of the 
artificial touch are invariant to intensity.
We extended this paradigm to arbitrary orientations and simple shapes, asking participants 
to identify the shapes evoked by two-PFs (3 or 5 shapes for c2 and c1, respectively). Both 
participants were able to correctly identify shapes on multiple digits (Fig.2C,D, c1:82±9–
72±8%, n=200, c2:50±14–48±8%, n=120, for d2-d3, p<0.05), though they were tended to 
confuse similar shapes (Fig.S3A). Next, we tested shapes composed of three PFs (Fig.S3B). 
c1 completed the task although slightly worse due to its greater complexity (60±22.7–
57±7.6% for d2-d3, p<0.05, n=160, Fig.2E, Fig.S3C), as observed in natural touch(16).
Finally, we examined the participants’ drawings of these perceived shapes (Fig.S3A, Fig.S4) 
and found that both the length and orientation of the evoked tactile shape matched our 
predictions from the single electrode reports (Fig.S3D–F). Participants would often report 
sensations of a continuous edge even when the individual electrode PFs were discontinuous 
(Fig.S3A,B, Fig.S4) an observation that highlights the similarity between the ICMS 2-point 
discrimination threshold and that of natural touch(19). This is also in line with the tactile 
funneling illusion(20, 21), where stimulation of multiple electrodes (like mechanical stimuli 
on the skin) can be spatially-integrated and cause a single cortical activation zone.
The results presented so far involve simple flat/planar sensations; however, most objects are 
rich with tactile features detected by cutaneous mechanoreceptors(22). We modulated the 
amplitude and timing of the stimulation of electrodes with adjacent PFs (Fig.3A) resulting 
in c1 reporting sensations of rounded edges (MovieS2). To test the precision with which c1 
could perceive this curvature, we modulated the amplitude to simulate changes in curvature. 
c1 could reliably identify (>75% success) curved stimuli once this amplitude difference 
exceeded 20% (Fig.3B), marginally worse than natural touch(23, 24), though performance 
depends on the contact area between the surface and finger. Above this threshold, c1 
could successfully identify randomly-presented concave, flat, and convex surfaces (Fig.S5, 
64±7%, n=75, p<0.05).
When we grasp an object, cutaneous inputs from multiple digits are integrated to form an 
object representation(25). To determine if our approach could be extended to support this 
complexity, we used mICMS that included PFs on multiple digits. Participant c1 reported 
sensations recalled from grasping real objects (MovieS3). Depending on their relative 
locations, the combination of three edges encoded on 9 electrodes with PFs on multiple

Spatiotemporal patterning of ICMS evokes sensations of tactile motion.
Static spatial information, as described above, could be used to improve object 
recognition for people with impaired somatosensation. However, stereognosis requires active 
manipulation and contact motion across the fingertips to build a rich object representation. 
In both vision and touch, information about motion is extracted from the spatiotemporal 
activation pattern across a two-dimensional sensory sheet (retina(26) and skin(3)). In 
natural touch, the perceived motion direction is determined by the ordered responses of 
a subpopulation of cortical neurons in BA1(3). We tested if we could mimic this effect by 
stimulating electrodes with adjacent PFs in a specific temporal sequence (continuous cortical 
trajectory) forming along proximal-distal (along the finger) and radio-ulnar (across the 
finger) axes (Fig.4A, Fig.S7A). Upon stimulation, both participants immediately described 
the evoked sensation as motion on their skin (MovieS4), and were able to distinguish the 
four motion directions (c1:76±14%, n=180, c2:78±15%, n=60; p<0.05, Fig.4A, Fig.S7A 
top), even when stimuli spanned multiple digits (c1:98±2%, n=120; c2:75±14%, n=75, 
p<0.05, Fig.4A bottom, Fig.S7B). We were able to evoke other apparent motions in 
participant c1, that included circular (96±7%, n=90, p<0.05) and radial motion (81±17%, 
n=75, p<0.05, Fig.S7D,E).
Next, we assessed the effect of ICMS duration and amplitude on the participants’ ability to 
determine direction of motion. Whereas duration had a significant effect (50ms–30%, 200ms 
–72%, n=60, p<0.05, Fig.S6A, with fixed inter-train-spacing), similar to natural touch(27, 
28), amplitude did not (p>0.05, n=60, Fig.S6B). Similar to the results on orientation 
described above (Fig.S2A), only a few hundred milliseconds were required to interpret 
motion direction, making this practical for closed-loop brain-computer interfaces (BCIs). 
Apart from their spatial arrangement on the skin, both edge orientation and motion encoding 
were achieved regardless of the specific characteristics of the individual PFs (quality, 
geometry, size, or perceived intensity).
To understand how multiple ICMS trains might influence motion percepts, we 
systematically varied their relative timing (negative or positive inter-train-intervals) and 
asked the participants to report whether a perception was continuous or pulsatile (Fig.4B). 
Perceived motion occurred only when trains were separated by 0 to 0.5 seconds. Any 
overlap caused them to appear to be a single event at two locations, whereas more than 
0.5 seconds between trains, typically caused the mICMS to be perceived as two successive 
events (intermittent motion(29)). This result occurred regardless of the PF separation, and 
the timings closely match those of natural touch(29, 30).

When we manipulate an object, neurons with the receptive field on the contact area start to 
fire and the object’s motion is encoded via temporal modulation of the firing(31, 32). To 
mimic this process, we investigated more effective dynamics of ICMS like those proposed 
for intracortical visual prostheses(33) and cochlear implants(34). In c1 we simultaneously 
stimulated three electrodes with aligned PFs and then modulated the voltage fields (via 
ICMS-amplitude) across these three electrodes (VFS, Fig.4C) moving the peak in the 
direction of the desired apparent motion. The direction of change of the current ratio 
across electrodes determines in which direction the pattern is “moving” on the cortex and 
how dynamic the pattern is perceived to be. With this spatiotemporal ICMS modulation, 
c1 was able to identify motion among four directions on the skin of d2 (c1: 74±12%, 
n=160, p<0.05, MovieS5) similar to sequential mICMS (NO-VFS, p>0.1). This encoding 
strategy might also evoke patterns of neural activation that are more similar to those 
generated by natural tactile motion. Information about direction and speed of motion is 
transmitted by local transients of neural firing modulated across neurons with adjacent 
receptive field(3), replicated here with modulation of mICMS amplitude. In the field of 
somatosensory neuroprosthetics, the use of more biomimetic approaches to stimulate the 
nervous system showed benefits compared to canonical strategies(10, 35, 36). This result 
demonstrates that this encoding method enables the simultaneous disentanglement of three 
distinct tactile features from ICMS: location, intensity, and motion direction.
The speed variation, that occurs during slip or surface exploration, is fundamental for 
tactile motion encoding(1). Hence, we varied the temporal activation of two adjacent PFs, 
and asked the participants to identify which of two mICMS patterns they perceived to be 
faster (Fig.4D, MovieS6). Both participants were able to discriminate motion speed with 
Weber Fraction=0.56±0.09, which falls in the same range as natural touch(37). Changing 
the onset lag between two equal-length ICMS trains caused the perceived speed to vary, 
when presented on both d2 and d3 in participant c1 (Weber Fraction=0.63±0.06, Fig.S8B). 
To ensure that the participants were identifying motion speed rather than train duration, we 
repeated the task using electrode pairs with different distances between PFs (Fig.S8A). We 
interleaved channel pairs with short (~0.6cm) and long (~1.4cm) spatial separation on d2 
in participant c1. There was no significant difference in point-of-subjective equality (PSE) 
for either the standard (500ms) or comparison (80–920ms) stimuli when presented for a 
given electrode pair. In contrast, ΔPSE was 360ms (PSE_speed=1.63cm/s) and −140ms 
(PSE_speed=1.67cm/s) for short and long PF separation, respectively, confirming that the 
effect is one of perceived speed, not train duration (Δdistance/ICMS_duration, cm/s). This 
speed ICMS encoding scheme resembles the afferent spatiotemporal code of motion on the 
skin surface(38, 39).
Tracing trajectories on the cortex allows complex tactile shapes to be encoded.
Motion is also an important component of tactile discrimination. Complex tactile patterns 
are easier to recognize when they are drawn on the skin (called graphesthesia) rather 
than simply indented(40) and active exploration of objects is more informative than 
static deformation of the skin(41). Similarly, shapes traced through the visual field using 
sequential mICMS within V1 are better recognized than using simultaneous mICMS(33). To 
test the effect of motion on complex tactile discrimination, we activated PFs representing

traced using the same approach.
Sequential activation also has the benefit of reducing the current injected into the cortical 
tissue at any given point, which might decrease sensory adaptation(33). With simultaneous 
stimulation, when the complexity of the tactile shape increases the resulting PFs can interact, 
often coalescing into a single PF that is not always described as a specific shape (Fig.5C). 
Sequential mICMS seems to diminish the likelihood of spatial blurring, improving the 
effective spatial resolution of the evoked patterns.
Finally, we tested whether these ICMS-based sensations could be exploited in the brain-
control of bionic arms in a real-time, functional task. We asked participant c1 to use 
a bionic arm via brain control to steer a wheel in response to movement perturbations 
(left, right, none) encoded via mICMS (Fig.5D, MovieS7). The participant was able to 
actively counteract the perturbations, exploiting only the ICMS-based sensations in real-time 
(No-CL:85±9%, n=40, p<0.01, Fig.5E). Moreover, participant c1 accomplished the task 
even with additional cognitive loads given by the simultaneous execution of mental tasks 
(Low-CL:80±12%, High-CL:50±21%, n=40, p<0.01, Fig.5E, MovieS7).
Discussion
This study advances our understanding of the effects of neurostimulation toward the goal 
of building better cortical neuroprostheses able to restore close-to-natural sense of touch. 
Despite the fact that BA1 neurons are often tuned to specific features (like orientation/
motion direction), ICMS has been typically used to evoke only simple tactile percepts, 
similar to the phosphenes evoked with cortical visual prostheses(42). This limitation is likely 
the result of activating all neurons in the electrode proximity regardless of their specific 
stimulus selectivity(43), resulting in a non-specific pattern. However, despite this, mICMS 
can be used to evoke coherent visual forms(44–46), and now coherent tactile percepts.
Our participants often perceived edges even when the individual PFs had spatial gaps. 
Illusion studies have revealed that top-down sensory expectations can modify the sensory 
experience(47), perhaps in this case to create a sense of continuity between individual PFs or 
the percept of motion from discontinuous PFs.
Our findings show that the perceived edge length correlated with the separation (cortical 
distance) between electrodes (Fig.S9A). This means that edge length and orientation 
are functions not only of the number of stimulated electrodes, but also of their spatial 
distribution. Furthermore, the direction of motion results from the cortical trajectories of 
mICMS-induced activity (Fig.S10A–C).
The sense of touch also allows three-dimensional shapes to be perceived(25). Tactile object 
perception includes the integration of information including contact points, curvatures,

texture and local edges that allows objects to be identified, even in the absence of vision(24). 
Previous studies have attempted to restore a sense of object shape, compliance or size 
using electrical stimulation of nerves(48, 49) and cortex(10, 50). Here, we advance our 
knowledge by exploring three dimensional shape encoding that leverages tactile features 
evoked on multiple digits. We hypothesize that when coupled with visual feedback and with 
active manipulation, these artificial sensations will result in even more natural and accurate 
percepts due to the additional context and the spatial processing capacity of the tactile 
system(15).
mICMS has previously been presented as a promising approach for improving other 
properties of artificial touch. Reductions in detection thresholds and reaction times have 
been reported(13, 51), as well as improved tactile discriminability and localizability(10, 
12). However, a potential drawback of mICMS is its effect on motor decoding; S1 ICMS 
directly trigger activity in the primary motor cortex (M1) (52) disrupting decoders. This 
disruption, however, can be minimized by implementing biomimetic neurostimulation, 
stimulus waveforms which emphasize contact transients over maintained contact(9, 53).
Stimulation hardware can also be substantially improved. Our S1 arrays consisted of a 
6×10 matrix electrodes, all of the same length, covering 2.4×4.0mm, thereby allowing only 
limited coverage of the cortical representation of the hand. Consequently, the combinations 
of perceptual edge lengths, orientations, and locations which can be evoked are severely 
limited. Closer electrode spacing would allow for more detailed maps of PFs that are 
necessary to restore acuity and richness closer to natural touch.
The fact that the digit representation is more accessible in BA1 than in other regions (like 
3b and BA2) with the approved microelectrode technology (Fig.S1B), made it an ideal 
candidate for BCI applications. Indeed, though we might achieve better spatial resolution 
by targeting the smaller Area 3b, its position deep in the central sulcus poses surgical 
and technological challenges. Furthermore, BA1, has larger, more complex receptive 
fields, typically encoding stimulus features that include global motion (vector average 
mechanism(3)) and shape. Moreover, in BA1, texture information carried in patterns of 
peripheral afferent activation are converted into a rate code by the spatial and temporal 
filtering properties of cortical neurons(54). Implementing specific spatiotemporal mICMS 
patterns could eventually allow us to encode complex sensations related to textures.
Finally, simultaneous recording of the evoked neural activity during these ICMS patterns, 
combined with the reported sensory experiences, could be a powerful tool to improve the 
prediction of the expected mICMS outcomes.
Although we successfully implemented a brain-controlled bionic arm with movement 
sensations, to expand the repertoire of artificial touch convey local edges and motion with 
mICMS, bionic hands require better sensorization, including smaller sensors and the ability 
to detect motion or tangential forces (using highly-sensorized artificial skin(55, 56)).
Despite the multidimensionality of tactile experience, we show that by using principled 
neuroscientific understanding of the neural encoding of conscious sensory experiences, it is 
possible to produce complex tactile sensations even within the limitations of existing neural
